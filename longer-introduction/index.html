1948 Friday 14 May 1948 · sixteen hours to midnight
The four blanks
Palestine·فلسطين·פלשתינה (א״י)

Friday, 14 May 1948. 8:00 a.m.

Sixteen hours.

At midnight tonight, thirty years of British rule in Palestine come to an end. The British High Commissioner leaves Jerusalem this morning. Nobody has been appointed to replace him.

You sit with the cabinet of a state that does not exist yet. Before sunset you have to agree on the words that will create it.

What comes next is up to you.

Read this before we play. It takes about seven minutes. Content note: this page mentions the Holocaust, and a war in which civilians on both sides were killed and driven from their homes.

Begin
The first of three papers

A letter

London, 2 November 1917

In 1917 Britain was at war with the Ottoman Empire, which had ruled Palestine for four hundred years. Five weeks before British troops took Jerusalem, the Foreign Secretary, Arthur Balfour, wrote to a leader of Britain’s Jews.

The promise in his letter is one sentence long. Thirty years of history came out of three of its phrases.

“a national home for the Jewish people”

Zionism was a movement, then twenty years old, which held that the Jews were a nation and needed a country of their own, in the land where their history began. Balfour’s letter put an empire behind it.

Look at the word he chose, though. He wrote home. He did not write state. For thirty years nobody could agree on what the difference was.

“in Palestine”

Palestine was not empty. When the British counted its people in 1922 they found about 750,000. One in nine was a Jew. Nearly all the rest were Arabs, most of them Muslim and some Christian, and they had lived there for centuries.

“nothing shall be done which may prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities”

That is how the letter describes nine out of every ten people in Palestine: by what they are not. It promises them civil and religious rights, and says nothing of political ones.

Britain had made two promises in one sentence. It spent the next thirty years finding out that it could not keep both.

What the letter did

Thirty years

Palestine, 1917 to 1947

1920s and 1930s

The home is built.

The League of Nations gave Britain a mandate: govern Palestine, and build the Jewish national home there. Under British rule Jews came by the tens of thousands. They bought land, founded towns and farms, and built a government in waiting, with its own schools, labor unions and militia.

After Hitler took power in Germany in 1933 they came faster. By 1939 there were 450,000 of them, almost a third of the country.

1936

The revolt.

Palestine’s Arabs watched a promised “home” turn into a state in the making, in a country they had expected to govern themselves. In 1936 they rose against British rule and against Jewish immigration.

It took Britain three years and a reinforced army to put the revolt down. Thousands of Arabs and hundreds of Jews were killed.

May 1939

The door closes.

Then Britain changed course. A war with Germany was coming, and it could not afford an Arab world in revolt. It limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 people over five years. After that, no more Jews could come unless the Arabs agreed.

Fifteen weeks later, Germany invaded Poland.

1945 to 1947

The survivors.

Six million Jews were murdered in Europe. When the war ended, a quarter of a million Jewish refugees sat in camps in Germany, Austria and Italy with no homes to go back to. Britain kept its limit, and the Royal Navy stopped the ships that tried to carry them to Palestine.

In July 1947 the navy boarded one of them, the Exodus, killed three people on board, and sent its 4,500 passengers back to Germany.

1946 and 1947

Britain gives up.

By then Jewish underground fighters were at war with the British as well. In 1946 they blew up British headquarters in Jerusalem’s King David Hotel and killed ninety-one people, British, Arab and Jewish.

Britain had 100,000 soldiers in Palestine, no money left after the war, and no policy that both peoples would accept. In February 1947 it handed the problem to the new United Nations. By autumn it had said it would leave.

The United Nations drew a map.

The second of three papers

A map

New York, 29 November 1947

The vote was 33 to 13, with ten abstentions. The United States and the Soviet Union, who agreed on almost nothing else that year, both voted yes.

The plan: two states, one Jewish and one Arab, and Jerusalem under UN rule. Britain had until August to leave. Both states were to exist by October 1948.

UN committee estimates. Counts of the Arabs inside the Jewish state run from 325,000 to about 407,000, depending on how the Negev Bedouin are counted.

The Jewish leadership said yes. Its state would come in pieces and would not include Jerusalem, but it would be a state.

The Arab leadership said no. Arabs were two thirds of the people of Palestine, they said, and the plan gave a Jewish third more than half the land. They said they would fight it.

The fighting began the next day. By spring it was a civil war, with the British packing to leave.

In April Jewish irregulars killed about a hundred villagers at Deir Yassin. Four days later Arab gunmen killed seventy-eight Jews, many of them doctors and nurses, on the road to a Jerusalem hospital. Before the Arab armies had crossed a border, at least a quarter of a million Palestinians had fled or been driven from their homes.

Tomorrow those armies, from five countries, are expected to cross it.

Six days ago the American Secretary of State told your foreign minister to postpone the state and accept a three-month truce. If the gamble failed, he said, do not expect rescue. On Wednesday your cabinet sat for thirteen hours and decided to go ahead.

Britain has printed its own account of the thirty years. It counts the farms, clinics and schools it built, and it blames its failure to set up self-government on “the mutual hostility of Arabs and Jews.” No UN commission has arrived to take over. Nobody is coming to hand you a country.

The third paper

There will be a Jewish state.
Nobody has agreed what kind.

Draft · not for publication

DRAFT DECLARATION OF INDEPENDENCE

For debate by the People’s Administration
14 May 1948

ERETZ-ISRAEL was the birthplace of the Jewish people. Here their spiritual, religious and political identity was shaped. Here they first attained to statehood and gave to the world the eternal Book of Books. After being forcibly exiled, the people kept faith throughout their Dispersion and never ceased to hope for their return and the restoration of their political freedom.

In the year 5657 (1897), Theodor Herzl convened the First Zionist Congress, which proclaimed the right of the Jewish people to national rebirth in its own country—a right recognized in the Balfour Declaration and re-affirmed by the League of Nations Mandate. The massacre of millions of Jews in Europe demonstrated the urgency of re-establishing the Jewish State. On 29 November 1947, the United Nations General Assembly passed a resolution calling for the establishment of a Jewish State in Eretz-Israel.

ACCORDINGLY WE, MEMBERS OF THE PEOPLE’S COUNCIL, ARE HERE ASSEMBLED ON THE DAY OF THE TERMINATION OF THE BRITISH MANDATE AND, BY VIRTUE OF OUR NATURAL AND HISTORIC RIGHT AND ON THE STRENGTH OF THE RESOLUTION OF THE UNITED NATIONS GENERAL ASSEMBLY, HEREBY 1 Statehood.

THE STATE will be open for Jewish immigration and the Ingathering of the Exiles; it will be based on freedom, justice and peace as envisaged by the prophets of Israel; it will ensure complete equality of social and political rights to all its inhabitants irrespective of religion, race or sex; it will guarantee freedom of religion, conscience, language, education and culture; and it will be faithful to the principles of the Charter of the United Nations.

THE STATE 2 Borders.

WE APPEAL to the United Nations to assist the Jewish people in building its State. WE APPEAL to the Arab inhabitants of the State to preserve peace and participate in its upbuilding on the basis of full and equal citizenship. WE EXTEND our hand to all neighbouring states in an offer of peace and good neighbourliness.

PLACING OUR TRUST IN 3 God, WE AFFIX OUR SIGNATURES TO THIS PROCLAMATION AT THIS SESSION OF THE PROVISIONAL COUNCIL OF STATE, IN THE CITY OF TEL-AVIV, ON THIS SABBATH EVE, THE 5TH DAY OF IYAR, 5708 (14TH MAY, 1948).

The name of this State shall be: 4 Name

The third of three papers

A draft

Tel Aviv, Friday 14 May 1948

This is the typed draft of the Declaration of Independence, as it lies on the cabinet table. Most of it is settled. Four blanks are not. Each blank is a fight, and each fight is over what kind of state this will be.

Blank 1 of 4 · Statehood

Do you wait for the UN?

The draft says you act “on the strength of” the UN plan. It still needs a verb.

  • “…hereby declare the establishment of a Jewish state.”

    Independence now, on your own authority. The American colonies did exactly that in 1776. But Washington has just asked you to wait.

  • “…hereby declare a provisional government in the framework of the UN.”

    A placeholder that keeps the UN and Washington on your side. But the UN’s timetable is already dead, and the armies are due tonight.

At the tableBen-Gurion, who chairs the cabinet, and Bentov want the first. Sharett and Rosen want the second.

Blank 2 of 4 · Borders

Where does it end?

The UN drew a map. The draft can accept it, ignore it, or go past it.

  • “No mention of borders.”

    The American Declaration named none. The army decides where the state ends, and every option stays open.

  • “…accepts the borders of UN Resolution 181.”

    The one map the world has agreed to. It also gives up Jerusalem and commits you to a state with a large Arab minority.

  • “…claims sovereignty over Jerusalem.”

    The holiest city, with 100,000 Jews inside it, under siege. The UN’s plan for it exists only on paper.

  • “…claims sovereignty over all of Eretz-Israel.”

    The Land of Israel, as the Bible has it. No map defines it, and every neighbor will read it as a threat.

At the tableRemez wants no mention. Kaplan wants the UN lines, so the Arabs inside them know whether they are citizens. Shapira and Bernstein want Jerusalem named.

Not a blank

“We appeal to the Arab inhabitants of the State to preserve peace and participate in its upbuilding on the basis of full and equal citizenship.”

Nobody at this table is Arab. The Arab leaders have refused the state. The people this line speaks to are leaving, or fighting, or about to become citizens of a country they did not choose, and the draft gives them no way to answer.

Your cabinet is not voting on this line. Read it anyway.

Blank 3 of 4 · God

Who do you trust?

The last line has the signers place their trust in someone. What goes there says whether this is a state for a nation, a religion, or both.

  • “The Rock of Israel.”

    An old phrase from Hebrew prayer. A believer hears God. A secular socialist hears the strength of the Jewish people. It works because it means two things.

  • “The Rock and Redeemer of Israel.”

    Unmistakably God. The religious ministers will sign gladly. The atheists will be asked to sign a prayer.

  • Leave it out.

    A state that makes no claim about God. The religious ministers will say that is no Jewish state at all.

At the tableZisling, a kibbutz farmer and an atheist, wants it out. Sheetrit wants God named outright. Golda Meir, who has no vote, defends the middle.

Blank 4 of 4 · Name

What is it called?

The draft ends with the name. It is the last blank, and the one most likely to be settled in a hurry.

  • “Israel.”

    The people’s old name. It ties the state to Jews everywhere, not only to those who live there.

  • “Judea.”

    The ancient kingdom. But on the UN’s map, most of the old land of Judea lies outside the Jewish state.

  • “Zion” or “Herzliya.”

    Names for the movement and for its founder, Theodor Herzl. They honor Zionism more than they describe a country.

  • “Eretz-Israel.”

    The Land of Israel, all of it. A name that claims more than any border.

AskWhatever you choose, ask who it leaves out. A state named for a people has to decide what to call everyone else.

The table

Ten ministers.

They belong to four parties, and the parties disagree about almost everything on that page. Golda Meir is in the room too, with a voice and no vote.

Labor

  • Ben-Gurion · Prime Minister, Defense
  • Sharett · Foreign Affairs
  • Kaplan · Finance
  • Remez · Transport

Socialists who built the Jewish community’s farms, unions and militia. They run it.

Marxist

  • Zisling · Agriculture
  • Bentov · Labour and Construction

Farther left. Kibbutz socialists, friendly to the Soviet Union and wary of religion in the state.

General Zionists

  • Rosen · Justice
  • Bernstein · Trade and Industry

Liberal and middle-class. Lawyers and businessmen who want courts, contracts and friends in the West.

Religious

  • Shapira · Health, Immigration
  • Sheetrit · Police, Minorities

A Jewish state must rest on Jewish tradition. Sheetrit also speaks for the Old Yishuv, the Jews who lived in the land long before Zionism began.

The cards

Every minister except Ben-Gurion holds one Obstruction card. Play it and all other business stops until your proposal gets a vote. Ben-Gurion holds the Hammer, which clears every Obstruction and forces a vote. Golda Meir holds the Compromise, which does the same with a middle-ground proposal.

The clock

The Sabbath begins at sundown, and the ceremony has to be over before it does. The British are gone at midnight. Nobody has promised you a minute more.

You have argued about this for months.

The arguing is over. Before sunset this cabinet has to put one text on the table and sign it.

Before midnight there will be a state of the Jewish people.

What comes after that, no one at this table knows.

Now read your role sheet.
